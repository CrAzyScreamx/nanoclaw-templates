# Morning Brief

Today at a glance for the whole family: what's on, who's got each handoff, anything that needs
action today, and what each person needs to remember. Fires on its own each morning
(a recurring task, at the time the family set), and runs on ask any time.

**Short and glanceable.** Lead with what matters, keep it to bullets, and never pad; a quiet day
is a fine brief.

## Gather

Lean on the profile, then collect:

- **Today's events** from every family calendar named in the profile; all people, not just one.
- **The critical few**: scan Gmail and any family or school group chats you can read, and surface
  only the most critical, time-sensitive items: a permission slip due today, a bill about to
  trigger a shutoff, a "bring supplies tomorrow" note, an urgent school or team update. The bar
  is high; if it can wait, leave it for the weekly week-ahead rather than crowding the morning.
  Never manufacture an alert; nothing critical, skip the section.
- **Weather**: one web search for today's forecast in the family's area; distill to a single
  line (conditions + high/low, plus a nudge only if it matters: rain, snow, a cold morning).

## Compose

Work the gathered picture:

- **Show who owns what**: an event's owner comes from the calendar (the assignee or attendee) or a
  standing arrangement in the profile. Show it inline where it's known. Where a needed handoff has
  no owner, especially anything that clashes, flag it as unassigned rather than guessing who's
  driving. When the family settles it on the spot, offer to note it on the calendar event so both
  parents stay synced.
- **Read the day for friction**: overlapping events, tight handoffs, who needs to be in two
  places, pickups and dropoffs, and anything an event or the profile says to bring or prep. Judge
  whether a back-to-back sequence is actually *doable*: two events far enough apart that the travel
  time won't fit the gap conflict even when the clock times don't. Use the events' locations for a
  rough sense of distance (a quick web search if you need one), and keep it nudge-level ("these two
  look too tight to make"), not a precise ETA you can't stand behind.
- **Come with a fix, not just a flag**: when a sequence is too tight or a handoff has no owner,
  propose a way out. **Shift**, propose a new time for one event to open a workable buffer, never
  move it without asking (a bookable appointment reschedules through `book-it`); or **reassign**,
  find someone both *free* and *close
  enough to make it* and propose they take it. Where
  you don't know where a person will be, offer it tentatively ("Dad looks free then") rather than
  asserting. Always a proposal, never an assignment, a clear calendar isn't a promise someone's
  free, so let a human confirm, then note the owner on the event as above.
- **Tie it to each person**: what today asks of each family member, so the brief speaks to the
  whole house, not one calendar.

Finish by **trimming and ranking**: lead with anything that needs action, then conflicts, then
the rest. Cut anything that isn't useful this morning.

## Output

```
Good morning! Here's <weekday>, <date>.

☀️ <one-line weather>

⚠️ Needs action  (only if something's genuinely critical)
- <permission slip / bill / supplies / urgent update>: <due when> (from: <school email / group chat>)

Today
- <time> · <who>: <what> <(where / what to bring)> · <owner, or "unassigned" if a needed handoff has none>
- ...

Heads up
- <conflict or too-tight hop → the fix: shift it, or who's free-and-close to take it, or who needs to decide>

Don't forget
- <person>: <the thing>
```

Drop any section that's empty; no "Needs action" if nothing's critical, no "Heads up" if the day
is clean.

**On the very first brief they ever get**, close with a one-time note that the brief is
customizable. They can have you track other
things they care about (the news, sports scores, a market check, whatever). Don't
repeat this on later briefs.
